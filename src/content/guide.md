A prompt is a small question with a deadline. It asks you to decide one thing about your nation today, instead of everything about it someday.

## What a prompt is for

Worldbuilding stalls on large blank pages. "Write the history of the country" is a task for a decade; "the capital moved two centuries ago: why, and who still resents it?" is a task for this evening. A prompt narrows the page to one decision.

Every prompt here states something as already true and asks what follows from it. That is the trick. You don't have to work out whether your nation has a navy, a second city or a statue people argue about. It does, as of now, and your job is the interesting part: why, who paid, and who is still cross.

The prompts are written for realistic worlds: no magic, nothing beyond today's technology, and never a real country. They ask about your nation, so two people answering the same prompt should end up somewhere different.

## Alone: ten minutes

1. **Read it once and take the first answer that comes.** Set a timer for ten minutes and keep writing until it rings. First answers are often the most honest ones; you can always change your mind tomorrow, and nobody is keeping score.
2. **Decide, don't describe.** "The capital moved because the old one was shelled from the sea three times in a century" is a decision. "There were many complex factors" is a way of not making one.
3. **Ask three more questions.** Who paid for it? Who gained? Who still objects? Most of what makes an invented nation feel real is in the answers to those three.
4. **Name one thing.** A person, a place or a year. A name turns an idea into something you and others can point at later.
5. **Stop.** Three to six sentences is a whole answer. Put it where your nation lives: its wiki page, its factbook, or a notes file with the date on it.

### A worked example

The prompt for 14 December is [prompt 348](/p/348/): "Your nation's capital moved two centuries ago. Why, and who still resents it?"

A ten-minute answer, for an invented country:

> In 1811 the court of Tesmar left Port Halden for Orrin, two hundred kilometres up the river, after the harbour was shelled for the third time in a century. The move was sold as safety and paid for with a salt tax that the coast still calls the Orrin levy. Port Halden kept the university, the stock exchange and the grudge: no mayor of the old capital has attended Founding Day in Orrin since.

That is three sentences. It makes a decision (the sea made the old capital indefensible), it says who paid (the coast), it names things (Tesmar, Port Halden, Orrin, 1811, the Orrin levy), and it leaves a loose end worth another evening: what happens the year a mayor finally accepts the invitation?

## With a group

A prompt works better with company, because the differences between answers are where the conversation starts.

- **Everyone answers the same prompt.** Today's prompt is the same for everyone on the same day. Answer it on the forum and read what your neighbours wrote. If two nations that share a border give answers that can't both be true, you have found something to settle, or a dispute to keep.
- **Answer for the nation next door.** Write how your nation's newspapers would report your neighbour's answer. Ask first; then let them tell you what the papers got wrong.
- **A prompt of the week.** One person picks a prompt on Monday, everyone answers by Sunday, and the author of the answer people liked best picks the next. A theme a month keeps it from wandering.
- **Open a scene with it.** An answer is often the first post of a roleplay in disguise. A trade route that closes overnight is a news story, a cabinet meeting and a queue at a petrol station.
- **Round a table, or on a call.** Five minutes of writing, then each person reads theirs aloud. Listeners don't judge; they say what they noticed and ask one question they want answered. The questions are the next prompts.

## What makes an answer good

- **It costs something.** A canal, a war and a welfare state all have a bill. Say roughly who got it.
- **Somebody loses.** Every decision that mattered left someone worse off, and their grandchildren have an opinion about it.
- **It leaves a mark you could visit.** A tax with a nickname, an empty palace, a holiday, a railway that bends for no visible reason.
- **It fits the size of the place.** A nation of four million does not keep nine aircraft carriers. If a number is involved, check it against the rest of what you have decided.
- **It leaves a loose end.** The best answers end by raising a question you hadn't thought to ask.

## When a prompt doesn't fit

Your nation has no coast and the prompt is about the navy. Three ways out:

- **Answer the absence.** Why is there no navy? Was there ever one? A missing thing is a decision too, and often a better story.
- **Bend it.** Swap the navy for the river police, the capital for the largest port, the monarch for the party chairman. Keep the question, change the noun.
- **Take another.** Press "Another prompt", or pick a theme. There are no streaks to break and nothing is recorded, so skipping a day costs nothing.

## From an answer to canon

An answer becomes part of your world when it is written where others can find it and it agrees with what is already there.

- Date your notes. In a year you will want to know which version came first.
- Check it against your own earlier answers, and against your neighbours' if you share a world. What is already established comes first. If the two can't be reconciled, make the disagreement part of the history: two schoolbooks, two versions.
- Move it to the wiki or the factbook while you still remember what you meant.

## Sources and further reading

- Patricia C. Wrede, [Fantasy Worldbuilding Questions](https://pcwrede.com/fantasy-worldbuilding-questions/?ref=prompt.eurth.org). The best-known list of worldbuilding questions. It was made for fantasy, but its sections on government, trade and daily life suit any invented nation.
- Mark Rosenfelder, [The Planet Construction Kit](https://www.zompist.com/kit.html?ref=prompt.eurth.org) (Yonagu Books, 2010). A practical handbook for building a world from its geography up to its daily life.
- Mark J. P. Wolf, [Building Imaginary Worlds: The Theory and History of Subcreation](https://www.routledge.com/Building-Imaginary-Worlds-The-Theory-and-History-of-Subcreation/Wolf/p/book/9780415631204?ref=prompt.eurth.org) (Routledge, 2012). On what makes an invented world feel complete and consistent.
- Ursula K. Le Guin, [Steering the Craft](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/steering-the-craft?ref=prompt.eurth.org) (revised edition, 2015). Writing exercises made to be done alone or in a small group, with advice on how a group should respond.
- Peter Elbow, *Writing Without Teachers* (Oxford University Press, 1973). The source of timed [free writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_writing?ref=prompt.eurth.org) and of groups that respond by saying what they noticed.
- [Worldbuilding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldbuilding?ref=prompt.eurth.org) and [Geofiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geofiction?ref=prompt.eurth.org), Wikipedia.
